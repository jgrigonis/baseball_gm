When an MLB player is injured, they are moved from the 26-man 
active roster to the Injured List (IL), freeing up a spot to 
call up a replacement, while usually remaining on the 40-man 
roster. Position players use a 10-day IL, while pitchers/two-way 
players use a 15-day IL, with both allowing for retroactive, or 
backdated, placement up to three days. 

Injured List Mechanics
10/15-Day IL: Players are removed from the 26-man active roster 
but still occupy one of the 40 spots on the 40-man roster.
60-Day IL: Used for long-term injuries. Players on this list are 
removed from both the 26-man and 40-man rosters, allowing the team 
to add a replacement player without needing to clear a spot through 
waivers or trades.
7-Day IL: Specifically for concussions.
Rehabilitation Assignments: Players can leave the team to play in 
the minor leagues to prepare for their return. 

Roster Management
Replacing a Player: To add a player to the 26-man roster, they must 
already be on the 40-man roster. If not, the team must 
"purchase their contract" and add them to the 40-man.
40-Man Limit: If the 40-man roster is full, a player must be removed 
via DFA (Designated for Assignment), trade, or by moving another 
player to the 60-day IL.
Retroactive Dating: IL stints can be backdated up to three days from 
the last game played to maximize efficiency. 

For example, if a pitcher is placed on the 15-day IL, the team can 
call up a player already on their 40-man roster to fill the 26-man 
spot immediately. If that pitcher is later moved to the 60-day IL, 
the team gets an extra 40-man spot for another player

In a typical baseball game, the risk of injury is relatively low, 
with roughly 5.78 injuries occurring per 1,000 athlete-exposures 
(one player participating in one game). About 25% of these injuries 
are severe enough to cause more than 10 days of missed

Injury Rates: Game injuries are 3 times more frequent than practice 
injuries.

Sliding causes 13% of injuries, while 10% result from being hit by 
a ball.

Pitchers and catchers generally face higher rates of injury, with upper 
extremity injuries causing the most significant time lost.

10-Day IL: The 10-day injured list (IL) is commonly used for 
short-term, acute injuries, such as muscle strains.

Pitchers in Major League Baseball (MLB) spend a significant and 
growing amount of time on the injured list (IL), accounting for 
over 70% of all days missed due to injury despite representing 
only a portion of the roster. Recent data indicates that MLB 
pitchers lost over 32,000 days to the injured list in 2023, 
with injuries increasingly concentrated in the shoulder and elbow. 

Key Statistics on Pitcher Injuries
Time Spent on IL: Pitchers are injured 41% more often than hitters 
and spend 144% more days rehabbing.

Total Days Lost: In 2023, pitchers accounted for 32,107 days on the IL.

Impact on Staff: Roughly 18,369 days were missed by pitchers due to 
throwing-related injuries in 2019, which is equivalent to roughly 
one-third of all opening day pitchers missing the entire season.

High-Volume Risk: Studies have shown that while about 27.5% of 
pitchers with at least five innings pitched appeared on the IL 
in 2023, some, such as those throwing complete games, have a 74% 
likelihood of landing on the IL.

Rising Trends: Pitcher IL days for elbow and shoulder injuries 
skyrocketed from 4,000 in 2010 to over 12,000 in 2024. 

Factors Contributing to Time on IL
Velocity: The pursuit of higher velocity is widely considered a 
major factor in the surge of UCL and shoulder injuries.

Age and Previous Injury: Many pitchers now enter the league having 
previously been hurt, with a high prevalence of pitchers having 
already undergone Tommy John surgery.

Early Season Impact: A large proportion of injuries now occur 
during Spring Training or in the first 30 days of the season. 

Pitchers typically miss 12 to 18 months following Tommy John 
surgery (UCL reconstruction), with many requiring at least a 
full year to return to competitive pitching. While rehabilitation 
often begins within 4 to 6 months, returning to full, 
high-intensity pitching capacity usually takes 14-16 months or 
longer.

non-pitchers frequently undergo Tommy John surgery (UCL 
reconstruction). While about 90% of these surgeries are 
performed on pitchers, position players—particularly catchers, 
shortstops, and outfielders—also require the procedure due to 
the high-velocity, repetitive throwing motions involved in the 
sport.

Approximately 30–40+ MLB pitchers undergo Tommy John surgery 
(UCL reconstruction) annually, with nearly 40% of all current
MLB pitchers having had the procedure at some point in their 
career. The frequency has risen drastically from 12 procedures 
in the 20 years between 1974–1994 to over 100 in single seasons 
recently, driven by higher pitch velocities and over-usage.
